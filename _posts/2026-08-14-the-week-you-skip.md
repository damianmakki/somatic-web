## Five good weeks, then an easier one

Five weeks into a block, the weights are moving well and you feel capable. Next week is a deload. You look at the reduced sets and wonder why you'd ease off now, just as things are going your way.

You'd rather keep going.

That reluctance is familiar to anyone who enjoys a good training run. The easy week can look like an interruption even when it's part of the plan that got you here.

Vladimir Issurin's landmark 2010 review in *Sports Medicine* on training periodization describes supercompensation — the phenomenon where performance gains from a hard training block are not fully expressed until after a recovery period.[^1] The hard weeks build the foundation. The deload is when the building actually happens.

<!--more-->

You won't see that recovery happening in the log the way you see another completed set. It still needs time.

---

## What "feeling fine" actually means late in a block

The objection is reasonable enough: you feel fine, you're making progress, and you have momentum. An easier week seems unnecessary.

The answer starts with understanding what accumulated fatigue actually looks like. Unlike acute fatigue — the tiredness after a single hard session that resolves with a good night's sleep — systemic fatigue builds across weeks and masks itself remarkably well. Your muscles adapt. Your perceived effort stays manageable. Performance continues, sometimes even improves slightly.

Meanwhile, your nervous system, connective tissue, tendons, and ligaments — structures that adapt and recover more slowly than muscle — are accumulating stress that doesn't announce itself as soreness. It announces itself later, as a plateau that appears without obvious cause, an injury that "came out of nowhere," or a performance crash that takes weeks to recover from rather than days.

Feeling fine is useful information. On its own, though, it doesn't tell you how much fatigue the block has built up.

---

## Keep the easier week easy

**Schedule deloads proactively, not reactively**

The instinct is to deload when performance crashes or pain arrives. By that point, you're recovering from a problem rather than preventing one. Evidence-based programming schedules a deload every 3–4 training weeks — not because performance has declined, but because the systemic recovery debt has accumulated to a predictable level based on volume and intensity. Proactive deloads cost you one easy week. Reactive deloads often cost two to four.[^2]

**Do the same Movements at reduced volume**

A deload is not a week of yoga, foam rolling, and cardio. It is a week of your regular training Movements at 40–60% of your normal set volume with similar or slightly reduced load. Same squat. Same bench. Same row. Half the sets. Maintaining movement patterns during the deload week preserves neural drive and technical skill. Switching to "active recovery" modalities doesn't accomplish this and reduces the benefit of the deload on the following block.[^3]

**Don't add volume elsewhere to compensate**

It's tempting to use the spare time for extra cardio or all the accessories you usually skip. That brings the total stress back up. Let the lighter week stay lighter across your training.

**Use the week to address weaknesses**

With fewer hard sets to get through, you have time to look at technique and mobility work that usually gets squeezed out. Keep it easy enough that it doesn't become another thing to recover from.

## What supercompensation actually looks like

A powerlifting-focused lifter runs a 4-week accumulation block with steadily increasing volume — 15, 17, 19, and 21 total sets per major movement pattern. Week five is the deload: 9 sets. Week six, they return to 15 sets.

Back at 15 sets in week six, the lifter reaches a training max on each main lift. The easier fifth week gave the lifter room to recover from the work of the first four.

Going straight into another hard block leaves less room for that recovery, with new fatigue added to what was already there.

---

An easier week can feel oddly unsatisfying. You leave the gym with more in reserve and fewer numbers to show for your time. That's the plan: arrive at the next block recovered enough to make use of it.

Judge the deload by how you train afterward, rather than how much work you managed to fit into it.
